A coolant leak, called an AC leak or refrigerant leak, is the escape of refrigerant fluid from a closed air conditioning system. It occurs through a crack, hole, or failed seal in the coils, copper lines, valve fittings, or compressor connections that make up the refrigerant circuit. The problem disrupts the pressure balance the system depends on to move heat out of a building. It worsens the longer it goes unaddressed. Parts of an AC system where a coolant leak forms

A coolant leak forms at any point in the sealed refrigerant circuit where a joint, wall, or fitting fails. The most common locations are the evaporator coil, the condenser coil, the copper refrigerant lines running between the indoor and outdoor units, the Schrader valves used for pressure testing, and the compressor fittings at the outdoor cabinet. Evaporator coil leaks are especially common in older systems because the coil sits in a humid airstream and is exposed to formic acid produced when certain air pollutants react with the aluminum fins. A process called formicary corrosion creates pinhole-sized breaches invisible to the naked eye. Condenser coil leaks are more often caused by physical damage, vibration fatigue at brazed joints, or UV degradation of the coil coating over years of outdoor exposure.

A coolant leak has several measurable attributes that technicians use to size the problem and choose the right repair. Leak rate is measured in ounces or pounds of refrigerant lost per hour, and even a slow leak of a few ounces per day will drop system capacity noticeably within a week. Pressure loss is tracked in PSI: a system running R-410A typically holds suction-side pressure near 120 PSI at normal operating conditions, and a drop below that range signals refrigerant loss or another restriction. Low suction pressure is a direct consequence of refrigerant loss and is one of the first readings a technician checks when a leak is suspected. Temperature change is the third key attribute. As refrigerant escapes, the evaporator coil loses its ability to absorb heat, so the temperature differential between supply and return air narrows from the normal 16 to 22 degrees Fahrenheit range toward single digits. Pressure differential across the metering device shifts, because less refrigerant mass is circulating through the expansion valve or orifice tube. Location of the leak determines whether a repair is a straightforward brazed patch or a full coil replacement, and rate of fluid loss determines urgency. A fast leak can empty a system in hours, while a slow leak may take months to show symptoms.

Signs of coolant leak in a residential AC system

Signs of a coolant leak include warm air from the supply registers even when the thermostat is set low, ice forming on the evaporator coil or the refrigerant lines near the air handler, and a hissing or bubbling sound near the indoor or outdoor unit. Cooling capacity drops noticeably. It worsens over days rather than appearing suddenly. A frozen coil is one of the most misread signs. Homeowners often assume the system is working too hard, when the actual cause is that low refrigerant pressure is dropping the coil surface below 32 degrees Fahrenheit and freezing the condensate that collects on it. Short cycling, where the compressor turns on and off more frequently than normal, is another sign, because the system cannot reach the thermostat setpoint and keeps restarting. Utility bills rising without a change in usage patterns point toward a refrigerant leak, since the compressor runs longer to compensate for lost capacity. How a coolant leak relates to the broader AC system

A coolant leak disrupts the entire air conditioning system because refrigerant is the working fluid that carries heat from inside the building to the outdoor condenser. Remove enough of it and the thermodynamic cycle breaks down at every stage. The compressor is the component most at risk from prolonged refrigerant loss: refrigerant vapor carries the lubricating oil that keeps compressor internals from overheating, so a system running low on refrigerant is running low on lubrication, and compressor failure follows. Coolant leak detection is therefore time-sensitive; a leak caught early is a repair, while one caught after compressor failure is a replacement. An air conditioning contractor uses electronic leak detectors, UV dye injection, and nitrogen pressure testing to locate the breach before adding refrigerant, because adding refrigerant to a leaking system without finding the source only delays the next failure. The opposite of a coolant leak is a sealed AC system holding a full refrigerant charge. A system in that state maintains stable suction and discharge pressures, consistent temperature differentials, and normal compressor run times. A coolant refill alone, without a repair, returns the system to a full refrigerant charge temporarily but does not address the breach, so the refrigerant escapes again and the cycle repeats. How to repair a coolant leak

The repair method depends on the location: a pinhole in a copper line is brazed shut, a failed Schrader valve core is replaced, and a corroded evaporator coil is typically replaced entirely because patching multiple pinholes in aluminum rarely holds long-term. After recharge, the technician verifies suction pressure, discharge pressure, and the supply-to-return temperature differential to confirm the system is operating within spec before closing the call.

Coolant leak detection methods

Coolant leak detection relies on four main methods, each suited to different leak sizes and locations. Electronic halogen detectors sense refrigerant vapor in the air around joints and fittings and can find leaks as small as 0.1 ounces per year, making them a standard first-pass tool. UV dye detection involves injecting a fluorescent dye into the refrigerant circuit, running the system, and then scanning with a UV light. The dye seeps out wherever refrigerant escapes and glows at the breach point. Nitrogen pressure testing pressurizes the evacuated circuit to a set PSI and monitors for pressure drop over time, confirming whether a leak exists even when the system is off and no refrigerant vapor is present. Soap bubble testing is the oldest method and still useful for large, fast leaks at accessible fittings. A technician applies a soap solution to joints and watches for bubbles as pressurized gas escapes. Combining electronic detection with UV dye gives the highest confidence on systems with multiple small leaks, which are common in older evaporator coils with formicary corrosion.
