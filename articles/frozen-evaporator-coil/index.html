Frozen evaporator coil in Tucson

A frozen evaporator coil, called an AC coil freeze-up, is an air conditioning condition in which ice builds up on the coil's tubing and fin plates. This ice blocks the coil from absorbing heat out of indoor air, causing the system to lose cooling capacity or shut down entirely. Coil temperature drops below 32°F when refrigerant pressure falls too low, airflow across the fins drops too far, or both conditions occur together. Left unaddressed, the ice layer thickens. Damage then spreads to the compressor. Parts of the evaporator coil and how ice forms on them

The evaporator coil consists of three main components: coil tubing that carries refrigerant, fin plates that transfer heat between the refrigerant and the air, and a thermal expansion valve that meters refrigerant flow into the coil. Ice starts on the fin plates nearest the return-air inlet, where airflow is slowest and moisture in the air contacts the coldest metal surface first. The process accelerates quickly. Once a thin layer of ice covers the fins, airflow drops further, the coil gets colder, and the ice spreads inward along the tubing. A fully iced-up AC coil can carry an ice layer 0.25 to 2 or more inches thick across the entire coil face. Evaporator coil cleaning addresses one frequent contributor to this condition.

Measurable attributes of a frozen evaporator coil

A frozen evaporator coil has several measurable attributes that distinguish it from a properly functioning coil. Coil temperature falls below 32°F and often reaches 20°F or lower when refrigerant charge is significantly low. Airflow through the coil drops by more than 40% of rated CFM as ice blocks the fin passages. Ice thickness varies from a thin frost layer at the early stage to a solid block exceeding 2 inches when the system has run frozen for several hours. Supply-air temperature rises at the same time because the coil can no longer absorb heat, so the thermostat keeps calling for cooling while the system delivers little or none. High head pressure is a related condition that can develop when a frozen coil restricts refrigerant flow back to the compressor, raising discharge-side pressure and stressing the compressor.

How a frozen evaporator coil fits into the broader AC system

A frozen evaporator coil is a fault within the broader air conditioning system, not an isolated part failure. The evaporator coil sits inside the air handler or furnace cabinet and works as the heat-absorption side of the refrigerant circuit. When the coil ices over, the refrigerant returning to the compressor is wetter and colder than designed, which can cause liquid refrigerant to flood the compressor and damage its valves. The condenser coil outside the home then loses the heat load it expects, and the entire refrigerant cycle runs out of balance. Catching an ice-covered evaporator early prevents a single coil problem from becoming a compressor replacement. A licensed technician diagnoses the root cause before thawing the coil, because thawing alone without fixing the underlying fault means the coil will freeze again within hours. AC not cooling frequently results when this fault remains uncorrected.

Common causes and signs of an iced-up AC coil

An iced-up AC coil develops from a short list of root causes: a clogged air filter that starves the coil of warm return air, a refrigerant leak that drops suction pressure below the freezing threshold, a dirty coil surface that insulates the fins from airflow, a failing blower motor that reduces CFM, or a stuck-closed thermal expansion valve that floods the coil with liquid refrigerant. Ice on the refrigerant line is the most visible sign. It often appears wrapped in condensation or frost at the service valve where the line exits the air handler. Other signs include warm air from the supply registers despite the system running, water pooling around the air handler as ice melts between cycles, and a hissing or gurgling sound from the refrigerant line. Airflow restriction from a dirty filter is a common cause in Tucson homes, where systems run for extended periods during the long cooling season. Frozen evaporator coil repair and the thaw process

Frozen evaporator coil repair starts with turning the system off or switching it to fan-only mode so the coil can thaw without the compressor running. Thaw time ranges from 1 to 24 hours. A coil carrying more than an inch of ice must thaw fully before a technician checks refrigerant charge or coil condition. Once thawed, the technician measures suction pressure, checks the filter and blower, inspects the thermal expansion valve, and tests for refrigerant leaks. Requirements can vary and should be confirmed with the EPA. A clear evaporator coil operating at its rated temperature, with full airflow across the fins and refrigerant pressure within the manufacturer's specified range, is the opposite condition of a frozen coil.
