Frozen evaporator coil in Tucson

A frozen evaporator coil, known as AC coil freeze-up, occurs when ice accumulates on the coil tubing and fin plates. The ice prevents the coil from absorbing heat from indoor air and causes the system to lose cooling capacity or stop completely. Coil temperature falls below 32°F when refrigerant pressure becomes too low, airflow across the fins decreases too much, or both happen at once. Without correction the ice layer grows thicker. This leads to compressor damage. Frozen evaporator coil repair on residential and light commercial systems counts as air conditioning repair. Parts of the evaporator coil and how ice forms on them

The evaporator coil contains three main components: coil tubing that carries refrigerant, fin plates that transfer heat between the refrigerant and the air, and a thermal expansion valve that meters refrigerant flow into the coil. Ice begins on the fin plates closest to the return-air inlet, where airflow moves slowest and moisture contacts the coldest metal first. The buildup speeds up rapidly. After a thin ice layer covers the fins, airflow decreases more, the coil temperature drops further, and ice moves inward along the tubing. A fully iced AC coil can hold an ice layer 0.25 to 2 or more inches thick over the entire coil face. Evaporator coil cleaning corrects one frequent contributor to this condition.

Measurable attributes of a frozen evaporator coil

A frozen evaporator coil shows several measurable attributes that set it apart from a properly functioning coil. Coil temperature drops below 32°F and often reaches 20°F or lower when refrigerant charge falls significantly low. Airflow through the coil decreases by more than 40% of rated CFM once ice blocks the fin passages. Ice thickness ranges from a thin frost layer in the early stage to a solid block over 2 inches after the system runs frozen for several hours. Supply-air temperature increases at the same time because the coil no longer absorbs heat, so the thermostat continues to call for cooling while the system supplies little or none. High head pressure appears as a related condition when a frozen coil limits refrigerant return to the compressor, which raises discharge-side pressure and stresses the compressor.

How a frozen evaporator coil fits into the broader AC system

A frozen evaporator coil represents a fault inside the larger air conditioning system rather than an isolated component failure. The evaporator coil resides inside the air handler or furnace cabinet and serves as the heat-absorption side of the refrigerant circuit. When the coil ices over, the refrigerant that returns to the compressor becomes wetter and colder than intended, which allows liquid refrigerant to flood the compressor and harm its valves. The condenser coil outside the home then receives less heat load than expected, and the full refrigerant cycle moves out of balance. Early detection of an ice-covered evaporator stops a single coil issue from turning into a compressor replacement. A licensed technician identifies the root cause before the coil is thawed, since thawing without correction of the root cause allows the coil to freeze again within hours. AC not cooling frequently occurs when this fault stays uncorrected.

Common causes and signs of an iced-up AC coil

An iced-up AC coil arises from a short list of root causes: a clogged air filter that limits warm return air to the coil, a refrigerant leak that lowers suction pressure below the freezing point, a dirty coil surface that insulates the fins against airflow, a failing blower motor that cuts CFM, or a stuck-closed thermal expansion valve that floods the coil with liquid refrigerant. Ice on the refrigerant line provides the clearest visible indicator. It frequently appears as condensation or frost wrapped around the service valve where the line leaves the air handler. Additional signs consist of warm air from the supply registers while the system runs, water that pools near the air handler as ice melts between cycles, and a hissing or gurgling sound from the refrigerant line. Frozen evaporator coil repair and the thaw process

Frozen evaporator coil repair begins by turning the system off or setting it to fan-only mode so the coil can thaw without compressor operation. Thaw time ranges from 1 to 24 hours. A coil that carries more than an inch of ice must thaw completely before a technician checks refrigerant charge or coil condition. After thawing, the technician records suction pressure, examines the filter and blower, inspects the thermal expansion valve, and tests for refrigerant leaks. Requirements can vary and should be confirmed with the EPA. A clear evaporator coil that operates at its rated temperature, with full airflow across the fins and refrigerant pressure inside the manufacturer-specified range, represents the opposite condition of a frozen coil.
