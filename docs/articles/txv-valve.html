Updated November 2026

TXV valve in Tucson

A TXV Valve stands for Thermostatic Expansion Valve and serves as a refrigerant metering device. It controls the amount of liquid refrigerant that flows into the evaporator coil, maintains evaporator superheat, and reacts to conditions in the suction line temperature and pressure. The valve changes its flow instantly to suit the cooling load by means of a mechanical assembly that includes a diaphragm, sensing bulb, needle, seat, and orifice. It is positioned at the evaporator inlet. The TXV belongs to the family of expansion valves, devices that reduce refrigerant pressure before the refrigerant enters the coil. Parts and scope of the TXV valve

The TXV valve is built around five main components that operate together as one mechanical feedback loop. The sensing bulb attaches to the suction line at the evaporator outlet and measures refrigerant temperature. This temperature information passes through a capillary tube to the diaphragm, which moves according to the pressure created by the bulb. The diaphragm then acts on the needle and seat assembly, widening or narrowing the orifice to increase or decrease refrigerant flow. A spring located beneath the needle supplies a constant closing force that establishes the base superheat value.

  • Diaphragm: Diaphragm movement converts bulb pressure into mechanical force on the needle, which makes it the valve’s main control element.
  • Sensing bulb: Sensing bulb placement on the suction line matters. Poor thermal contact produces unstable superheat readings and erratic system behavior.
  • Needle and seat: Needle and seat geometry controls the valve’s flow precision; a worn seat lets refrigerant leak past at low load.
  • Orifice: Orifice size determines the valve’s maximum flow capacity, rated in tons of refrigeration.
  • Spring: Spring tension is the adjustable element in an externally equalized valve, set at the factory and sometimes modified in the field during startup.

The TX valve connects to the evaporator coil on its outlet side and to the liquid line on its inlet side. An external equalizer port, found on most residential and light-commercial valves, connects to the suction line past the bulb so that pressure drop across the evaporator does not affect the superheat signal. Thermal expansion valve selection influences how effectively these connections keep proper superheat under different loads.

Measurable attributes of the TXV valve

Several measurable attributes determine TXV valve performance and must align with the system it serves. Choosing a valve outside these ranges leads to either starved or flooded evaporator operation, both of which lower efficiency and risk compressor damage. AC System Diagnostics frequently starts by comparing these exact attributes to manufacturer specifications.

  • Superheat control range: Superheat control range for a correctly adjusted TX valve usually stays between 8°F and 12°F at the evaporator outlet under design conditions, although some manufacturers aim for as low as 5°F on high-efficiency coils.
  • Flow capacity: Flow capacity is rated in tons of refrigeration, with residential valves commonly offered from 1.5 tons to 5 tons and light-commercial valves extending past that range.
  • Refrigerant flow rate: Refrigerant flow rate is given in BTU/hr and must correspond to the evaporator coil’s rated capacity; a valve undersized by even half a ton starves the coil and increases suction pressure.
  • Pressure range: Pressure range across the valve inlet and outlet changes with refrigerant type. R-410A systems run at significantly higher pressures than older R-22 systems, so valves are refrigerant-specific and cannot be interchanged.
  • Temperature sensitivity: Temperature sensitivity of the sensing bulb decides how fast the valve reacts to load shifts; a bulb with poor contact or insulation lag produces hunting, in which the valve moves back and forth between open and closed instead of holding steady superheat.
  • Connection type and size: Connection type and size are given in inches for both the inlet (liquid line) and outlet (distributor or coil inlet) fittings, with common residential sizes running from 3/8 inch to 7/8 inch OD.

How the TXV valve fits into the central air conditioning system

TXV valve operation forms an essential part of the four-component refrigeration cycle. Refrigerant exits the condenser as high-pressure liquid and arrives at the TX valve, where the pressure drop turns it into a low-pressure mixture that absorbs heat in the evaporator coil. The compressor then draws that vapor, compresses it, and returns it to the condenser to release heat outdoors. The TX valve serves as the control point that keeps the evaporator completely active while stopping liquid refrigerant from reaching the compressor. Flooding occurs when the valve lets liquid refrigerant reach the compressor. A properly operating central air conditioning system relies on the TX valve keeping superheat inside its target range under every load condition, from a mild Tucson spring morning to peak summer demand above 110°F.

The TX valve works directly with the air handler, the refrigerant type, and the line set length. Line set length counts. Longer line sets raise pressure drop and can change the valve’s operating point, sometimes calling for field adjustment of the spring tension. Refrigerant charge and TX valve calibration affect each other. A system that seems undercharged may actually contain a restricted or failed valve, and incorrect diagnosis results in unnecessary refrigerant additions that make the issue worse. AC Refrigerant Recharge procedures therefore confirm TXV function before any refrigerant is added.

TXV valve vs. Fixed orifice tube

TXV valve systems receive frequent comparison with systems that use a fixed orifice tube, known as a fixed orifice, which serves as the main alternative metering device in residential air conditioning. A fixed orifice consists of a simple plastic insert with a calibrated hole; it delivers refrigerant at a constant rate no matter the load, suction temperature or outdoor conditions. The TX valve changes its opening continuously, which suits it to variable load conditions and high-efficiency equipment. Fixed orifice systems are simpler and less costly to build, yet they cannot hold tight superheat control over a broad range of operating conditions. Most modern high-SEER equipment employs a TX valve or an electronic expansion valve instead of a fixed orifice, since tighter superheat control raises measured efficiency. When replacing a coil or air handler, verifying whether the new assembly requires a TX valve or a fixed orifice forms a required step. Combining a fixed-orifice coil with a TX-valve-equipped condenser, or the opposite, creates performance and reliability issues. For systems nearing the end of their service life, a related service to consider is AC unit replacement, where the metering device matches the new equipment from the beginning.

Common problems and signs of a failing TXV valve

TXV valve failure appears through several measurable indicators. A technician can detect them with gauges and a thermometer. Superheat that stays consistently high, above 15°F to 20°F at the evaporator outlet, signals a restricted or stuck-closed valve that starves the coil of refrigerant. Superheat that stays low or drops to zero signals a stuck-open or flooded valve that allows liquid refrigerant to move toward the compressor. Hunting, in which suction pressure fluctuates repeatedly instead of remaining steady, indicates a sensing bulb that has lost its charge or maintains poor contact with the suction line.

  • Ice on the evaporator coil: Ice on the evaporator coil forms when refrigerant flow is too low and the coil surface drops below freezing, a direct result of a restricted TX valve.
  • Warm air from supply registers: Warm air from supply registers despite the compressor running points to a starved coil, which is one of the most common field presentations of a failed TX valve.
  • Compressor short-cycling: Compressor short-cycling under normal load can result from a flooded valve sending liquid refrigerant to the compressor, triggering high-pressure cutout.
  • Erratic suction pressure: Erratic suction pressure that swings more than a few PSI over a short observation period is a sign of sensing bulb failure or loss of bulb charge.
  • High superheat with correct charge: High superheat with a confirmed correct refrigerant charge is the clearest indicator that TXV valve replacement is needed rather than additional refrigerant.

Adjusting TXV valve spring tension is sometimes tried before replacement, but external adjustment only changes the superheat set point. It cannot fix a mechanically failed diaphragm, a contaminated needle seat, or a bulb that has lost its sensing charge. Work is performed under Arizona ROC contractor license for HVAC. Frozen Evaporator Coil issues frequently trace back to TXV valve faults identified during this diagnostic process.

TXV valve replacement in Tucson

TXV valve replacement is a refrigerant-side repair that requires recovering the system charge, reaching the liquid line connection at the evaporator inlet, brazing or flare-fitting the new valve in place, re-insulating the sensing bulb against the suction line, recharging the system, and confirming superheat at the new valve’s rated set point before the job ends. Requirements can vary and should be confirmed with the EPA. The replacement valve must match the original in refrigerant type, flow capacity in tons, connection size and equalizer type. An externally equalized valve cannot replace an internally equalized one without changing performance. In cases where the evaporator coil itself has degraded, replace the TXV valve and coil together to prevent a second refrigerant recovery and recharge cycle, which increases labor and cost. When the overall system has aged, a full AC replacement may be more cost-effective than replacing separate refrigerant-side components on a system beyond its service life.
